## Research Agenda

My research examines how organized economic interests and mass publics shape economic policy, and how citizens hold governments accountable for the outcomes. I work primarily with survey and conjoint experiments, often fielded across multiple countries, combined with observational data on firms, elections, and policy.

**Business, Interest Groups, and Economic Policymaking** — Firms are increasingly mobile, and governments compete to attract and retain them. This line of work asks when that competition pays off politically and when it distorts policy. I study how electric utilities and renewable energy producers lobby across federal systems, how governments design incentives for private investment, and how the domestic political interests of weaker states shape what they concede in trade agreements. A recurring theme is that the political logic of courting business often diverges from its economic returns.

**Public Opinion in Global Politics** — Foreign economic policy is made under the constraint of public opinion, but citizens rarely encounter trade, aid, or investment in the abstract. Using survey experiments across East Asia, South Asia, and Africa, I study how the identity of a trading partner, the framing of an aid project, or the conduct of a foreign government shapes what people are willing to support. Much of this work examines opinion in aid-receiving and middle-income countries, where the literature has been thinnest.

**Political Economy of Environment and Energy** — Energy and climate policy distributes costs unevenly and immediately, which makes it a demanding test of democratic accountability. I study whether voters punish governments for disasters and price shocks they did not cause, and why fuel subsidies persist despite their well-documented inefficiency. A parallel strand examines electricity access and power sector reform across developing countries.
